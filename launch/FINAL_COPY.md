## Figma Community file listing

**African Icon Library — 30 icons for Nigerian life**

Search any major icon set for "danfo" and you get a generic bus. For "suya", a kebab. The objects
that fill an ordinary Nigerian day have no icons, so interfaces built here borrow shapes that mean
something else.

This is the beginning of a fix. 30 icons across food and drink, transport, commerce, culture,
music, textiles and identity — every one drawn on the same 24-pixel grid with the same stroke
logic, and every one checked at 16 pixels before release.

One weight, `regular`. Thin, bold and fill are specified and not drawn; a weight is a drawing, not a
stroke-width change, and this library will not pretend otherwise.

MIT licensed. Corrections welcome — especially from people who know these objects better than we do.

## Figma plugin listing

**African Icon Library**

Insert any of 30 icons for Nigerian life directly into your file as editable vectors — danfo, okada,
suya, talking drum, calabash, naira sign and more. Search by name, filter by category, insert at any
size.

Works entirely offline. The plugin bundles every icon at build time, declares
`"allowedDomains": ["none"]`, and its build fails if any networking call reaches the bundle. It
sends nothing anywhere.

MIT licensed. Free, and free to stay that way.

## LinkedIn — launch post

Search any major icon library for "danfo".

You will get a bus. A generic, grey, side-on bus — the same one you get for "transit", "coach" and
"shuttle". Not a yellow Lagos minibus with a conductor hanging off the back. Search "suya" and you
get a kebab. Search "gele" and you get nothing at all.

This is not a gap in taste. It is a gap in the record. Interfaces built in Lagos borrow shapes that
mean something else, because the shapes that mean the right thing were never drawn.

So we drew some. The African Icon Library is 30 open-source icons for Nigerian life, on one
24-pixel grid, MIT licensed.

What I want to be honest about is what it is not. It is 30 icons, not a complete system. It has one
weight, and the other three are specified and undrawn — because a weight is a drawing, not a
stroke-width change, and faking it would make the set a lie. Two drawings were withdrawn from this
release last week for failing to read at 16 pixels, which is why it is 30 and not 32.

Nigeria first. The continent is the roadmap, not the claim.

If we drew something wrong, tell us. That is the whole contribution model.

## LinkedIn — founder essay

**What it takes to turn a market stall into an interface**

An icon is an argument about what is worth pointing at.

Every icon set encodes a world. Open any of the big ones and you will find a mailbox with a little
red flag — a piece of suburban American hardware that most of the planet has never touched — and
you will not find a jerry can, though half the world's fuel moves in one.

That is not malice. It is proximity. People draw what is near them, and the sets that became
universal were drawn near San Francisco.

The consequence is small and constant. A Lagos fintech building a payments flow reaches for a
credit-card icon because there is no icon for a POS agent's terminal, though the agent is how the
money actually moves. The interface ends up slightly foreign in a way nobody can name.

Fixing it turns out to be harder than deciding to fix it. A photograph of a danfo is not an icon. A
danfo is yellow, striped, dented, crowded, loud — and an icon is a shape that survives being
sixteen pixels wide. Most of what makes a danfo a danfo is the first thing to disappear.

So the work is not drawing. The work is deciding what a thing is when you have removed almost
everything about it, and being willing to throw away the drawings where that decision came out
wrong. We threw away nine: seven that read as the wrong object, and two more last week that read as
nothing at all at 16 pixels.

30 survive. That is the whole set, and the number is not the point — the standard is.

## Cultural-review recruitment

We are looking for Yoruba, Igbo and Hausa speakers to check some names.

The African Icon Library is 30 open-source icons of everyday Nigerian objects. Each carries local
names in the metadata, and **not one has been confirmed by a speaker yet** — they are marked
unverified, because saying otherwise would be a claim we have not earned.

One drawing is held back entirely: our own audit could not confirm what the object is, and
releasing it would assert a name nobody has verified.

The task is small and concrete: look at an icon, tell us whether the object is what we say it is
and whether the name is right. There is an issue template for it. Corrections outrank feature work
in this project, and contributors are credited.
